# Series: pricing creative work

Her idea, July 2026. A short series on pricing services, opening with why pricing a service
is harder than pricing a product.

Voice, cadence and format from `linkedin-content-system.md`. Drafting from
`.claude/skills/linkedin-writer/`.

---

## Verdict: worth doing, with one reframe that is not optional

**Why it is worth doing.**

1. **It solves the problem we kept hitting.** The Aug 3 pricing post went through three
   drafts and still read thin. It was thin because it was trying to be an entire subject in
   one post. Her voice rule is one idea per post taken seriously, and pricing is not one
   idea. A series lets each instalment be the right size. **The drafted post becomes
   instalment two rather than a lonely standalone.**
2. **Almost nobody senior does this in public.** Designers avoid talking about money. A
   creative director of 22 years being clear and unembarrassed about it is genuinely rare.
3. **Dual-audience, and unusually strong on both sides.** At home it reads as commercial
   maturity and business development leadership, which is exactly the read she wants while
   holding a Senior Art Director title. For EU recruiters, **commercial judgement is the
   thing that separates a Creative Director from a senior designer** in European agency
   hiring. Agencies hire creative leads who can defend a number to a client.
4. **It has zero relocation signal**, provided the reframe below holds.
5. **It feeds CIM and CIM feeds it.** Pricing strategy, value-based pricing and perceived
   value are core Level 7 marketing theory. `02-cim-level-7/module-tracker.md` explicitly
   says to look for overlaps where coursework doubles as other work. This is one.

## ⚠️ The reframe: agency side, never freelancer side

**"How to price your services" is the signature content genre of someone about to go
independent.** It is what people post in the six months before they leave to freelance. Her
employer reads this profile.

That is not a relocation tell, but it is the same category of problem: content that signals
exit. And it is entirely avoidable.

| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| How to charge what you are worth | How institutional buyers evaluate a number |
| Stop undercharging | Why the cheapest proposal usually wins, and when it does not |
| Know your value | What a committee is actually comparing |
| Raising your rates | Where the number sits in a proposal |

**The test:** does the post read as advice to a freelancer setting rates, or as an agency
practitioner explaining how institutional pricing actually works? The second is safe,
senior, and more interesting. The first is a flare.

Everything below is written to the second register.

## The other hard rule

**No real figures, ever.** No client budget, no margin, no fee, no tender value, no day
rate, hers or the agency's. Not disguised, not rounded, not "a project in the low six
figures." Pricing content is the one subject where a commercially sensitive detail can slip
out while sounding like craft.

The series works entirely on mechanism rather than amounts, which is also what makes it
readable.

## Shape: five posts, finite

Finite matters. September carries the IELTS sitting and the attestation chain, and an
open-ended series that stalls in public is worse than no series. Five instalments, announced
as a few, delivered.

**Mondays, roughly fortnightly, interleaved with her normal mix.** Consecutive instalments
would make the feed monotonous and would concentrate the pattern change. Spaced out, it
reads as a thread she returns to.

## Instalment 1: why pricing a service is harder than pricing a product

**Hook,** 88 characters, physical, and it uses her actual material rather than an abstraction.

> I can tell you what a book costs to print. I cannot tell you what the idea for it costs.
>
> A product can be priced backwards. It cost this much to make, it sells for that much, the
> gap is the business. Anyone can check that arithmetic, including the person buying.
>
> A service has no such arithmetic. What it cost me to make is hours, and hours are the worst
> proxy there is for what the work is worth. The times I have worked fastest were the times I
> knew most, which is to say the times the work was best. Price by the hour and you are
> charging for your own inefficiency.
>
> Underneath that there is a harder problem. When I put a number in a proposal, the thing the
> number refers to does not exist. The client is not buying a design. They are buying a
> description of a design, written by the person who would go on to make it, and they have to
> decide what that description is worth before anyone can look at it.
>
> That is an act of trust rather than an act of comparison. Which is why price behaves so
> strangely in this work. A cheap product is a bargain. A cheap service makes people nervous,
> and they are usually right.
>
> I am going to write a few of these over the next couple of months. Pricing is the thing
> designers are worst at discussing in public, and it costs us more than any craft problem I
> know.

**Alternative hooks, measured:**

| | Hook | Chars |
|---|---|---|
| A | The thing does not exist when you name the number. That is the whole difficulty with pricing a service. | 103 |
| B | You can price a product by adding up what it cost to make. Try that with a service and you lose money for years. | 112 |
| C | A cheap product is a bargain. A cheap service makes people nervous, and they are usually right. | 95 |

C is the sharpest line in the post and is currently doing better work in the closing
movement than it would at the top.

**Image.** The book is in the hook, so use it: **a printed book or a curriculum spread,
physical, in her hands or on a desk.** She has this material already, unlike the proposal
photographs the other drafts need, which makes instalment one the easiest to ship.

**Tag.** None needed. This one carries itself.

### Checklist

**Voice.** No em dashes. No bullets, no tip list, no CTA. One idea. Fragments as rhythm.
The series announcement in the final paragraph is a statement of intent rather than a
request, so it does not breach the no-CTA rule.

**Substance.** No external claims, nothing to verify. "The times I have worked fastest were
the times I knew most" is her experience and should be confirmed as true before it goes out,
since it is stated as a general fact about her career.

**Register check.** Reads as an agency practitioner explaining institutional pricing, not as
advice to a freelancer setting rates. No figures anywhere.

**Not a repeat.** Distinct from the July proposal post, which was about structure and order
and never mentioned money. Still needs the check on "22 years in the room" and "Q2
reflection", which remains outstanding.

**Two-reader test.** *As her boss:* a senior creative thinking publicly about commercial
craft, with no client detail, no figures and no freelancer register. Reads as someone
growing into the commercial half of the role. Passes. *As an EU recruiter:* commercial
judgement at director level, which is the line between a creative director and a senior
designer in European agency hiring, and almost nobody demonstrates it in public. Passes
strongly.
