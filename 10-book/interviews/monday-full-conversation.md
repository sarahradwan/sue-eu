Notes from a Life I Didn’t Plan
Some of it happened to me. The rest, I happened to.
Sara R.
Introduction
My mother used to brag about me like I was some kind of emotional support child. The quiet one. The obedient one. The peaceful, yes-saying daughter who never caused a fuss.
“Sara, eat your lunch.”
“Yes, Mama.”
“Sara, time to go to bed.”
“Yes, Mama.”
“Sara, let’s go home.”
“Let’s go, Mama.”
Always yes.
From day one, I was agreeable—not because I didn’t have opinions, but because, even then, I think I knew arguing was useless. I wasn’t raised in a democracy. It was easier to survive by staying silent, avoiding conflict, and keeping things calm.
That doesn’t mean I was weak. It means I was strategic.
But let’s be clear: deep down, I’ve always had a louder version of me living in the back of my head.
She doesn’t speak much.
But she observes. Everything.
When I was three years old, barely knee-high, my mum raised her hand to hit me for something wrong I’d done.
I snapped.
I pointed my tiny finger at her and said, “Don’t you ever hit me again.”
She didn’t.
She never did again.
Years later, in my twenties, she was the one who told me that story. She’d bring it up whenever I forgot who I really was—the other Sara. The one who surprised her. The one who surprised me when I first heard about it, too.
Anytime I started spiralling or shrinking myself, she’d pull out that memory like it was proof.
“You’re the girl who stood up for herself at three. So don’t act like frightened prey.”
She passed away four years ago. But honestly? She’s still narrating half my confidence.
Fast-forward through almost a decade of mess: losing my dad, barely surviving a toxic relationship, and living through a country in flux.
The revolution came, shook everything, and then quietly faded—and with it, a lot of the hope I had pinned to staying.
By 2013, Egypt felt like a shirt I’d outgrown. Still mine, still loved, just too tight to wear every day.
Cairo’s congestion was part of it; the city inhales eighteen million people and forgets to exhale.
I needed oxygen that hadn’t already been breathed.
So I accepted a promotion and moved to Qatar.
I landed in Doha at dawn. The sky was a rinsed-out blue I recognised from childhood summers on the Mediterranean and Red Sea coasts. Egypt can do pristine when it’s not being the centre of everything. But here, the colour came without the diesel aftertaste.
Skyscrapers stood quietly, mirroring that blue instead of screaming for attention.
No one leaned on a horn.
No one leaned on me.
The air tasted refrigerated, almost sweet, as if the city had been unwrapped from plastic five minutes earlier.
I rolled my suitcase across a marble arrivals hall that echoed with… nothing.
People minded their own business the way you only can when no one expects you to be anybody’s business in the first place.
I stepped outside, filled my lungs, and felt the clamp around my ribs loosen.
Egypt still owns my best stories. Doha just let me breathe long enough to tell them.
Apparently, that decision made enough noise.
Plenty of Egyptians were leaving back then, but not many women were doing it alone, for work, with no male signature to validate the choice.
A reporter asked questions. I answered. A line or two ended up in a major American newspaper.
I wasn’t trying to make a statement.
I just needed air.
Qatar gave me that—at least at first.
No men yelling at me in the street. No weird comments. No casual harassment. Just women walking around like they belonged.
I was stunned.
And for a while, I let myself belong, too.
At work, I thrived. The editor-in-chief was calling me directly about my designs. I felt seen, powerful—like the version of me I’d buried years ago finally got her moment.
But of course, the story doesn’t end with a happy montage.
I was still a Middle Eastern woman in a male-dominated office. Which meant success came with a sarcastic side of sabotage.
The stronger I got, the more fragile some of the men around me became.
Passive-aggressive comments. “Second wife” offers. Rumours about my reputation as a single Middle Eastern woman.
The whole insecure male circus.
I couldn’t cry at management’s door every time one of them felt threatened. I was too busy trying to do my job while pretending I didn’t hear half the things they said.
Eventually, I moved again.
Dubai.
I built a business, made a fortune, lost it.
(We’ll get to that.)
But through all of it, I kept moving forward.
And no, I didn’t find myself.
She was never really lost.
She just needed permission to stop surviving and start showing up.
And that’s what this book is about.
I didn’t write it because I figured everything out. I wrote it because I didn’t—and because I know you probably haven’t either.
It’s not a guidebook.
There are no morning routines. No glowing green juices. No ten-step plans to optimise your life.
Just real stories from a woman who spent years saying yes to everything—and is finally learning how to say no.
So if you’ve ever felt like you were holding your life together with coffee, screenshots, and blind optimism…
Welcome.
You’re not alone.
Now let’s get into it.
Chapter One
I’m Not Lazy, I’m Spiritually Horizontal
The manager leaned back in his chair, arms crossed, and said it like he was diagnosing a medical condition.
“Sara, you’re good at what you do. But you’re so slow. You see your colleagues hopping around the office like frogs!”
I smiled. Said nothing.
But I laughed in my head, imagining the office as a pond and the manager as a Goliath frog with a golden crown.
I went home and wondered if he was right.
Am I the turtle or the frog?
While trying to find a good place to work, I spent three weeks at what I can only describe as a rabbit-hole newspaper.
The owner was completely nuts, giving impossible tasks, expecting me to read her mind and finish whatever encyclopaedia-long instructions she had in fifteen minutes.
And if I didn’t, the screaming was pure torture.
And just when I thought it couldn’t get worse, there was him.
A colleague who could’ve doubled as a statue of fragile masculinity.
Back then, I wasn’t great with one of the graphic design applications, but I was acing another—the industry standard for newspaper and magazine layouts.
He was using Adobe Illustrator for newspaper layouts.
Well, it could be done, sure.
But you’ll suffer.
I asked him to help me learn some Illustrator basics.
“Search the internet,” he said, with a mocking smile.
That was it.
No support. No guidance.
Just a pure I will not help you flag planted firmly in the ground.
I’ll give it to him—he was fast. Compared to me, anyway.
I like to take my time. I need to understand the situation, the brief, really see what’s needed before I act.
And then I polish.
But because of my slower pace, he decided I was lazy.
“You’re too laaaaaazy.”
Stretching the word to exaggerate.
Not just him. I’ve heard it from colleagues, from friends, throughout my life.
And I denied it publicly. Of course I did.
But deep inside? I was acting as if it were a fact.
I am so slow. Deal with it.
As if it were something carved into my DNA that I just had to accept.
In another between-jobs stint, I worked as an admin at some company—I’ve honestly forgotten what industry it was.
Two weeks in, I already knew this wasn’t it.
When I resigned verbally (because there was no HR… duh), the manager told me:
“Sara, you’re good. But you’re so slow. Your colleague runs around in her heels fetching papers. When I call her, she’s here in less than a minute. When I call you, you take a few minutes just to get up and walk over.”
I swear I did not sign up to be a marathon athlete at that company, whatever it was.
And that newspaper?
I stayed three weeks.
Then made a fast decision to leave.
Not so slow, I guess.
Because here’s the thing about my pace: when it’s crisis time, it completely flips.
I act.
I decide.
In minutes.
When I found out my mum had been diagnosed with cancer, my sisters were completely lost.
Weeping. Confused. Not knowing where to go or what to do.
I went straight to the CEO and asked him to let me work remotely for a couple of months.
Surprisingly, he agreed.
(My line manager refused, but the CEO overruled her.)
I booked a ticket to Cairo the next day.
Flew out.
Took my mum to multiple doctors. Made sure she was on track with her treatment protocol.
And then I started a rhythm I’d maintain for months: working from overseas, supporting my mum, flying back to Dubai monthly to finalise the magazine and send it to print, managing client relationships, keeping everything running.
Three or four days in Dubai.
Then back to Cairo.
And somewhere in the middle of all that—the hospitals, the protocol, the fear—something shifted in me.
My mum’s cancer didn’t just teach me how to act fast.
It healed my social anxiety.
I know that sounds strange.
But before that, I was terrified of people.
Of speaking up. Of managing conversations, confrontations, negotiations.
I’d freeze.
Overthink.
Disappear into myself.
But when your mum is sick and you’re the one coordinating doctors, trying to understand medical jargon, arguing with insurance, making your way through bureaucracy, making life-or-death decisions—you don’t get to let anxiety make the decisions anymore.
You just act.
And after?
I couldn’t go back to being that silent, frozen version of myself.
I could speak now.
Manage.
Act.
Be present with people in a way I never could before.
I became different because of it.
My mum used to remind me:
“You’re the girl who stood up for herself at three. Don’t act like frightened prey.”
And maybe that’s what I’d been doing all those years in those jobs.
Acting like prey.
Moving slowly not because I was deliberate, but because I was scared.
Scared of being wrong.
Scared of being seen.
Scared of taking up space.
But when she got sick, I remembered who I actually was.
Not the girl people called lazy.
The girl who pointed her finger and said, “Don’t you ever.”
That girl doesn’t move slowly because she’s weak.
She moves slowly because she’s choosing her steps.
And when it matters?
She runs.
Acting fast when threatened, I think, is a natural instinct.
Being able to activate it?
That’s the hard part.
But I can.
And I do.
Maybe my slow-paced lifestyle did protect me when I lost my mum. When I lost my business.
Maybe it helped me create a buffer, a way to process the world without drowning in it.
It’s a strange thing—moving slowly while everyone else is on fast-forward, and somehow, it doesn’t affect you.
I’m not sure if it’s a virtue.
But what I am sure of is that everybody’s different.
Those people jumping around like caffeinated frogs every day?
They’re living their lives fully, just like I am.
I’m not slow.
I’m deliberate.
I’m not lazy.
I’m careful.
And when the world is on fire?
I’m the one who saves the people I love without breaking anything important along the way.

Working Chapter: Women for Women
The Work Permit
Before leaving Egypt to work abroad, Sara needed a government work permit.
At the government office, a male employee could not categorise her Applied Arts qualification in the same way he recognised conventional professional degrees such as medicine or engineering.
He wanted a man's signature or approval.
At that point, Sara was in her late twenties. Her father had passed away. She had no brothers, no husband, and no uncles on either side of the family.
She spent the day being sent from one employee to another, trying to get the document approved.
Eventually, another woman intervened.
The woman asked whether Sara's faculty was similar to engineering.
Sara agreed enthusiastically. After all, who said Applied Arts wasn't engineering?
The woman smiled, took her papers to the manager, and helped persuade him that the qualification could be treated as equivalent. She also implied that Sara had connections—wasta—to help get the paperwork through.
The permit was granted.
What makes this story worth keeping: Not the bureaucracy alone, but the unexpected moment when another woman recognised the absurdity of Sara's predicament and chose to help. It belongs in a chapter about women supporting women, not in the introduction.
To develop: The appearance and tone of the office, the precise exchange at the window, what Sara felt after hours of being redirected, and the moment the helpful woman appeared.
