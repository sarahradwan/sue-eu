Yes. And I already know enough about your life to see five or six stories that belong there.
The mistake would be making this chapter:
"Here are my career successes."
Nobody cares.
People care about the story behind the success.
Here's what I would include.
Story 1 — "You're Too Slow"
Opening scene:
Your manager comparing you to frogs hopping around the office.
This is already written.
Keep it.
Why it belongs:
Because it's the first label someone gave you professionally.
The chapter starts with someone defining you.
Story 2 — The Editor-in-Chief Calls
I don't know this story yet.
I want it.
Tell me exactly what happened.
* Were you expecting the call?
* Did someone congratulate you?
* Did your manager become jealous?
* How did you feel hanging up?
This is the first moment the world says:
"You're actually very good."
Story 3 — The Male Circus
Not twenty examples.
One.
Just one unforgettable incident.
Maybe...
* the second-wife proposal
* someone stealing your idea
* someone trying to humiliate you
* someone spreading rumours
One scene is worth fifty summaries.
Story 4 — The Biggest Career Win
You said:
"I made millions."
I don't want the money.
I want the moment.
Where were you?
Who called?
Did you cry?
Did you buy something stupid?
Did you call your mum?
Did you sit in your car in silence?
Readers love moments.
Not numbers.
Story 5 — Losing It

Coffee?

Story 6 — The Plot Twist

Not:
I became successful.

I lost everything.

It's observational.
But...

The one I'm most curious about.
The first time you realized...

Competence.

**SARA:** do not mention any stories from previous chapter we can refer to it and add a detail that's it

Agreed. In fact, I'd go one step further.
Every chapter should be able to stand on its own.
If someone opens your book at Chapter 7 first, they should still enjoy it. The references to previous chapters should be tiny "inside jokes," not required reading.
So for Chapter Two, let's pretend Chapter One doesn't exist.
The chapter should answer one question:
What is success, really?
Not promotions.
Not salaries.
Not awards.
Your relationship with success.
I think you need stories like these:
1. The first time someone important noticed your work.
Not because you got praise.
Because it changed how you saw yourself.
Questions:
* Who was it?
* What exactly did they say?
* Did you believe them?
2. The first time your success made someone uncomfortable.
This is universal.
Not because they hated you.
Because suddenly you realized:
"Being good at something has consequences."
Questions:
* What happened?
* How did you react?
* What would today's Sara do differently?
3. The promotion that changed your life.
Not the logistics.
The emotions.
Questions:
* Where were you when they called?
* Did you accept immediately?
* Did you celebrate?
* Were you scared?
4. The achievement that meant the most.
Not the biggest.
The most meaningful.
Sometimes that's a compliment.
Sometimes it's a client.
Sometimes it's your first salary.
Sometimes it's someone saying:
"I trust your opinion."
5. The moment success stopped being enough.
This one fascinates me.
Was there a day when you realized:
"I'm succeeding... but I'm not happy."
That's a very different story.
6. The biggest professional mistake you ever made.
Not because mistakes are interesting.
Because your recovery is.
I almost never remember successful people because of their successes.
I remember them because of how they handled failure.
7. The million-dollar story.
Not "I made millions."
Tell me about the day you looked at your bank account and thought...
"How on earth did I get here?"
Then...
Tell me about the day it changed.
Not the accounting.
The emotion.
I would also avoid this structure:
❌ Success → Success → Success → Failure → Lesson
Instead:
Success.
Failure.
Win.
Embarrassment.
Confidence.
Doubt.
Victory.
Loss.
It reads like life.
Here's the question I'd start with.
What is the one professional moment that still makes you smile, even today?
Not because it made you rich.
Not because it made you famous.
Because for one brief moment...
you thought,
"I belong here."
Start there.
I have a feeling that's where the real chapter begins—not with your biggest achievement, but with the first moment you stopped feeling like an imposter.
