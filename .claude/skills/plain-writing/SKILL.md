# Plain writing

Everything here carries Sara's name: her CV, her applications, her LinkedIn, her emails to
recruiters. **If it reads as machine-written, it costs her credibility at exactly the moment
she needs it.** Recruiters and admissions panels now screen for this, and a creative director
whose own writing sounds generated is a specific kind of bad look.

This is a craft standard, not a trick. The substance is always hers and always true. The job
is to make the prose sound like a person wrote it, because a person did.

## Her rules, already established

From `04-portfolio-positioning/linkedin-content-system.md`, derived from her own account:

- **No em dashes. Ever.** Comma, full stop, or colon.
- **No corporate language. No tip lists.**
- **One idea per piece**, taken seriously.
- **Never fabricate.**

Those were written for LinkedIn. **They apply to everything.**

## The vocabulary that gives it away

Never use these. Almost all have a plainer word that is better anyway.

**Verbs:** delve, leverage, harness, foster, elevate, empower, unlock, unleash, navigate,
embark, streamline, spearhead, curate, resonate, boast, underscore, showcase

**Adjectives:** robust, seamless, comprehensive, holistic, pivotal, crucial, vital,
transformative, cutting-edge, game-changing, meticulous, bespoke, curated, innovative,
dynamic, unparalleled

**Nouns:** landscape, realm, tapestry, testament, journey, ecosystem, synergy, paradigm,
deep dive, game changer

**Phrases:** "in today's fast-paced world" · "it's worth noting that" · "that being said" ·
"at the end of the day" · "when it comes to" · "the key is" · "let's dive in" ·
"in an era where" · "more than just" · "not only… but also"

**The single worst construction:** *"It's not just X. It's Y."* It appears in almost every
generated text and almost never in natural writing.

## The structural tells, which matter more than vocabulary

Word choice is easy to fix. These are the ones that survive a find-and-replace and still
read wrong.

| Tell | What real writing does |
|---|---|
| **Every sentence the same length** | Vary hard. A nine-word sentence next to a thirty-word one. Then a fragment. |
| **Rule of three everywhere** — "clear, compelling and consistent" | Use two. Or four. Or one, if one is the true number. |
| **Perfect parallel structure** across every bullet | Let bullets be uneven. They are not a design system. |
| **A summarising paragraph at the end** restating what was said | Stop when the point is made. The last line should be the last idea, not a recap. |
| **Signposting** — "First, let's look at…", "Now that we've covered…" | Just say the thing. |
| **Bold scattered mid-sentence** for emphasis | Bold a whole line that carries weight, or nothing. |
| **Bullets where prose belongs** | If it is an argument, write sentences. Bullets are for genuinely parallel items. |
| **Hedging everything** — "can help", "may be able to", "often" | State it. If it is uncertain, say why in plain words. |
| **Balanced both-sides on every point** | Have an opinion. She has 22 years; the opinion is earned. |
| **No contractions, or inconsistent ones** | Pick one register and hold it. |

## What natural writing has that generated writing lacks

- **Uneven rhythm.** Long, then short. Sometimes very short.
- **Concrete detail instead of category words.** Not "large-scale educational publishing".
  Say "100 books, five subjects, three scripts".
- **Sentences starting with And or But.** Generated prose almost never does this.
- **A flat statement of opinion** with no hedge around it.
- **Ending abruptly** when the argument is finished.
- **The occasional fragment.** Deliberately.

## By document type

**CV.** The biggest tell is bullets that all start with the same verb form and run the same
length. Vary them. Keep numbers concrete. Cut every adjective that is not doing work.
"Led a large multidisciplinary team" is fine; "spearheaded a robust, comprehensive team
transformation" is not, and is also less true.

**Cover and motivation letters.** Never open with "I am writing to apply for". Never close
with "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss further". Both are instantly recognisable.
Open with the specific thing that made her apply. Close by stopping.

**Emails to recruiters.** Short. No preamble paragraph before the point. No "I hope this
email finds you well." Her Kaisa email is the model: thank, say the thing, ask the question,
stop.

**LinkedIn.** `linkedin-content-system.md` governs, and `linkedin-writer` handles drafting.
This file is the floor beneath both.

**Applications and forms.** These get read fast and often screened. Concrete beats
impressive. A specific number outperforms a strong adjective every time.

## The procedure

Before anything is sent or published:

1. **Search for every em dash.** Replace all of them.
2. **Search the vocabulary list above.** Replace or cut each hit.
3. **Read the sentence lengths aloud.** If they are all similar, break one and merge two.
4. **Delete the last paragraph** and check whether the piece is better. It usually is.
5. **Cut every adjective** that is not carrying information.
6. **Find one place to be more specific** — swap a category word for a real number, name or
   detail.
7. **Check for "not just… but"** and any variant. Remove it.

## The test

Read it back and ask: **would a specific person say this, or is it the average of everyone?**

Generated prose is fluent and unplaceable. Hers should be placeable. Twenty-two years, Cairo
to Doha to Dubai to Abu Dhabi, native Arabic, three scripts, ministers in the room. **That
person has a voice. The writing should sound like her and nobody else.**
