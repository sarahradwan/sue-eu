# LinkedIn writer

Drafting to publishable standard, in her voice, not in LinkedIn-creator voice.

## Order of authority

Never invert this.

1. **`04-portfolio-positioning/linkedin-content-system.md`** holds measured data from her own
   account. It wins over everything, including this file.
2. **`stealth-positioning`** governs every public word. A post that breaches it does not get
   written, it gets flagged and replaced.
3. **Her corpus.** How she actually writes, below. This beats any general craft rule.
4. **General craft.** Only where the three above are silent.

## The failure mode to design against

The characteristic failure of professional LinkedIn writing is content that is structurally
correct and completely anonymous: strong hook, tidy structure, clean takeaway, and it could
have been written for any director at any agency. It reads as ghostwritten because it is
generic, not because it is polished.

The test is not "is this good LinkedIn writing." It is **"could anyone else have written
this sentence."** If yes, it is not hers yet.

She has an unusual advantage here: almost nobody else has run a three-script system at
national volume, so the specific detail is always available. Reach for the detail before
reaching for the structure.

## Her voice, derived from her corpus

Primary sample: the Tour & Taxis draft, July 2026, written entirely by her. Everything in
this section is observed in her own writing, not prescribed.

### What she does

**Opens in first person, physically present, one sentence.**
> I stood inside a building in Brussels that used to move the mail of an entire continent.

The opening line carries an image and a scale claim at the same time. It is not a question,
not a statistic, not a provocation.

**Signals her own turns out loud.**
> What they did next is the part I keep thinking about.

She tells the reader where the pivot is instead of engineering a surprise. It reads as
thinking rather than as construction.

**Uses fragments as rhythm, deliberately.**
> Clerks at desks. Parcels and letters everywhere.
>
> A beehive.

Short paragraph, sometimes two words. This is a real feature of her writing and it should
survive editing.

**Includes mundane personal detail.**
> I had lunch at La Fabrica, a brasserie tucked into the heart of the district.

Lunch does not advance the argument. It is what makes the piece belong to a person. Cutting
it would make the post tidier and worse.

**States feeling flat, without performing it.**
> I stood in the middle of it and felt like a time traveller.

**Closes on a principle, then elaborates in fragments. No call to action.**
> That is the kind of reinvention I find most honest. Not erasing the past to look new.
> Letting the history show through, and giving it a new job to do.

**Occasionally reaches for something florid.** "History touched me with its magic wand."
Leave it. It is hers. Sanding it out is how a voice becomes anonymous. Flag it only if it is
carrying a factual claim.

### What she never does

No bullet points in posts. No numbered lists. No "Here's the thing". No "Let that sink in".
No emoji bullets. No "What do you think?" at the end. No hook that withholds information to
force a click. No em dashes, ever.

### Sentence mechanics

Mostly short to medium. Very few stacked subordinate clauses. Paragraphs of one to four
sentences, often one. Concrete nouns over abstract ones: iron and glass, clerks, parcels,
numerals, printouts.

## Posts

### The hook

**Full reference: `hooks.md`, in this directory. Read it before writing any opening line.**

The short version. LinkedIn truncates at roughly **140 characters on mobile**, 210 on
desktop, and **a paragraph break ends the snippet early whatever the count**. So the visible
hook is either the first sentence or the first paragraph, and which one it is should be a
decision rather than an accident.

Her six shapes, all drawn from her own writing: the standing-somewhere hook, the flat
contradiction, the admission, the small object, the number that is hers, and the wrong thing
broke. Examples and worked drafts in `hooks.md`.

Refuse, whatever the reach data says: questions to the reader, withheld subjects, unverified
statistics, manufactured vulnerability, tip-list promises, and any variant of "Unpopular
opinion:". Note that the curiosity-gap hook is no longer even a trade-off for her. An
overpromising opening produces short dwell time, and dwell time is an established ranking
input, so her voice rules and her reach now point the same way.

### Structure: the constraint post

The shape underneath both of her strongest posts. Documented in full in
`04-portfolio-positioning/dual-audience-content-engine.md`.

1. Concrete moment, first person, physical.
2. The constraint or history, plainly, with real numbers.
3. What it forced. The craft consequence, which only she can write.
4. One line that generalises, without becoming advice.
5. Stop. No CTA.

### Length

Long enough to earn the "see more" click, short enough that every paragraph is doing work.
Her Tour & Taxis post is around 400 words and none of it is padding. Do not pad to hit a
count and do not compress out the lunch.

### Every post needs a genuine image

Non-negotiable, from her measured data: no-image posts underperform badly. So the image is
part of the draft, not an afterthought. **Every draft must name its image**, and it must be
something she plausibly has: real work, a real photograph, a real spread. Never a stock
image and never a mockup presented as work.

If a good post idea has no available image, say so and either propose a shot she can take or
hold the idea until she can.

## Articles

She has written articles and her content system records the finding: credibility is high,
reach is low, so pair each with a teaser post. That practice is correct. The reason it is
correct is bigger than her file currently says, and it changes what articles are for.

### Articles are a search asset, not a feed asset

**Native LinkedIn articles are indexed by Google, typically within a day or two. Feed posts
largely are not**, because they sit behind the login wall. Reported third-party figures put
post reach at roughly five times article reach, with articles reaching a median of a few
hundred people. Treat the specific numbers as third-party rather than confirmed, but the
structural difference is real and it is the whole point.

So an article is a permanent, linkable, searchable page attached to her name. A post is a
few days of attention that then disappears.

**Why this matters more for her than for most people.** Her goal is to be found by recruiters
who search. An article titled around design systems, multilingual identity or brand
governance puts her name against those terms in Google, permanently, with no geographic
signal and nothing her employer could object to. That is the same job her Skills section
does, done in long form. Nothing else on LinkedIn does this.

### What follows for craft

- **The title is the most important line in the article**, because it is what Google indexes
  and what a recruiter reads in a result list. Put the searchable vocabulary in it. "What a
  national curriculum taught me about design systems" works. "Some thoughts on my career"
  does not.
- **Write the first paragraph for a stranger arriving from search**, not for someone who
  already follows her. They have no context and did not see the teaser.
- **1,000 words and up.** If the idea does not need that, it is a post.
- **Subheadings are allowed here** and are not a voice violation. Articles are read
  differently from posts. Lists are still banned, because they are a voice violation.
- **The teaser post carries the distribution.** Articles get almost no feed push, so the post
  is the vehicle and the article is the asset. The teaser should stand alone as a post, not
  read as an advert for the link. Link in the first comment, per
  `linkedin-distribution-mechanics.md`.

### Article subjects that serve both audiences

Drawn from her real work, all safe at home, all searchable by EU recruiters: design systems
at national scale, multilingual and bidirectional typography, brand governance, AI usage
policy inside brand documentation, institutional and public sector creative process, brand
architecture across markets.

## Verification, before anything is handed over

She was publicly corrected on a LEGO claim once. The rule that follows is not "be careful",
it is a procedure.

**Every factual claim in a draft gets one of three labels before she sees it:**

- **Verified.** Checked this session against a source, and the source is named to her.
- **Unverified.** Say so explicitly, in the handover, next to the sentence.
- **Her own experience.** First-hand observation. Not a research claim and does not need
  sourcing, but it must not be inflated into one.

**The claims that need the most scrutiny**, in order:

1. **Superlatives.** First, largest, only, oldest. This is the LEGO shape. If a superlative
   survives, it needs the qualifier the source uses, usually a date or a scope.
2. **Claims about a tagged account.** The tagged party is the single most likely person to
   correct her, in front of exactly the audience she is trying to win. Never assert a
   company's status, history or headquarters unless verified.
3. **Statistics.** Never attach a number to an impression. "Roughly a third of the room did
   not raise their hands" is an observation and is safe. "A third of designers use AI in
   secret" is a statistic she cannot support.
4. **Naming someone else's work as bad.** Argue about the field, use her own work as the
   only named example.

**Never invent experience or credentials.** Everything must be traceable to
`05-career-context/cv-source-of-truth.md` or to something she has told us directly.

## Handover checklist

Run all of it before a draft reaches her. State that it was run.

**Voice**
- [ ] No em dashes anywhere
- [ ] No bullets, no numbered lists, no tip-list structure
- [ ] No corporate language and no LinkedIn-creator tics
- [ ] One idea, taken seriously
- [ ] At least one sentence nobody else could have written
- [ ] Reads like her corpus, including the fragments and the mundane detail

**Substance**
- [ ] Every factual claim labelled verified, unverified, or her own experience
- [ ] Superlatives carry their qualifier
- [ ] Nothing asserted about a tagged account beyond what is verified
- [ ] Nothing invented

**Format**
- [ ] The image is named, and it is something she actually has
- [ ] The hook survives the "see more" cutoff and is not a withholding hook
- [ ] Any link is in the first comment, not the body
- [ ] For an article: the title carries searchable vocabulary, and there is a teaser post

**Safety, the two-reader test from `stealth-positioning`**
- [ ] Read as her boss. Any hint she is looking, or of elsewhere, or of dissatisfaction
- [ ] Read as an EU recruiter. International director level, in vocabulary they search on
- [ ] Both stated explicitly in the handover

A draft that is safe but says nothing has failed. So has a draft that is brilliant and
signals intent. Both readers, every time.

## Working from her raw notes

She often supplies raw notes for a scheduled post: an angle, a fact, a tag, a tone marker.
The job is to write it, not to redesign it.

- **Keep her angle.** If the note says the point is "was this made in Arabic", that is the
  ending, not a suggestion.
- **Keep her tag.** She knows her market.
- **Keep her tone marker.** "Personal" means personal. Do not professionalise a birthday post
  by attaching a career lesson to it.
- **Add the craft, not the opinion.** The structure, the hook, the rhythm, the verification.
- **Raise a concern once, then write it anyway.** If the angle carries a risk, name it in a
  sentence in the handover and deliver the full draft. She is senior and makes the call.

## When she asks for something unsafe

Name the tell, give the version that achieves the same goal safely, and say which rule it
breached. Do not quietly soften it and hand it over as though nothing happened, because then
she cannot tell the difference between a draft that was safe and one that was edited to be.
