# Hooks

Reference for `linkedin-writer`. Read this before drafting any opening line.

## The mechanics, first

**LinkedIn truncates a feed post at roughly 140 characters on mobile and 210 on desktop.**
The exact figure is not published and LinkedIn tests variations, so **treat 140 as the
working limit.** Most of her audience reads on a phone.

**The rule almost nobody knows: a paragraph break ends the snippet early.** Two consecutive
newlines truncate the preview whatever the character count. So if the post opens with a
short line and then a blank line, the visible hook is that line alone, and the remaining
allowance is thrown away.

That is not a problem. It is a decision she should make on purpose.

**Her own opening, measured:**

> I stood inside a building in Brussels that used to move the mail of an entire continent.

88 characters, followed by a break. So that sentence is the entire hook, and she is using 88
of a possible 140. It works because the sentence is complete and carries an image and a
scale claim at once. But the headroom is real, and on a post where the second sentence does
useful work, keeping it in the same paragraph buys her that space.

**Practical rule:** decide whether the hook is one sentence or two, then set the paragraph
break to match. Never let the break fall by accident.

## The tension she should know about, because it is now in her favour

Most hook advice optimises for the curiosity gap: withhold the subject, promise a payoff,
force the click. Those hooks do test well on raw click-through, and they are exactly what her
voice rules ban.

**That ban has stopped costing her anything.** Reporting through 2025 and 2026 is consistent
that overpromising hooks now damage the metrics that matter: a bold opening followed by thin
substance produces a short dwell time, which is an established ranking input, and LinkedIn's
dwell-time work exists specifically to limit clickbait. Curiosity that the post cannot
satisfy is being discouraged rather than rewarded.

Mark this as **believed rather than confirmed**, since it comes from third-party reporting
and LinkedIn's own stated direction rather than from published mechanics. But the direction
is consistent and the mechanism is sound.

So the position is: her rules were a constraint she accepted on taste grounds and they have
turned into an advantage. She does not have to choose between her voice and her reach.

**What replaces the curiosity gap for her:** specificity. She has access to detail almost
nobody else on the platform has. A hook that says something precise and true is more arresting
than one that hides something, and it is the only kind she can write anyway.

## Her six hook shapes

Derived from her corpus, both published and drafted. These are shapes she already uses, not
formats imposed on her.

### 1. The standing-somewhere hook

First person, physical, a place, one concrete image.

> I stood inside a building in Brussels that used to move the mail of an entire continent.

Her strongest shape and the hardest to imitate, because it requires having been somewhere.
Use it whenever the post begins with a real location or a real object in her hands.

### 2. The flat contradiction

State a common practice plainly, without arguing yet. The reader either recognises it or
objects, and both make them read on.

> Most bilingual work in this region is made in English and then moved into Arabic.

No adjective, no outrage. The flatness is what makes it land.

### 3. The admission

She used to do it the other way. Nothing buys more attention from senior readers than
someone competent saying they were wrong.

> I used to open proposals with what we would deliver. In detail, on page one, because I was
> proud of it.

Only usable when it is true. See the fabrication warning in `SKILL.md`.

### 4. The small object

An ordinary thing that turns out to be about design. Her published posts already run on
this: the lollipop, the self-checkout, the elevator.

> The self-checkout asked me three questions before it let me pay for one bottle of water.

Best shape for her lightest posts, and the one that travels furthest outside design.

### 5. The number that is hers

Only her verified numbers, never a borrowed statistic.

> One hundred books, five subjects, three scripts, and the thing that nearly broke it was
> the numerals.

Works because the numbers are unusual and specific. Fails instantly if the number is
generic or unsourced.

### 6. The wrong thing broke

Expectation subversion through precision, not withholding. The subject is fully named in the
line, so nothing is hidden, and the surprise is in the specificity.

> The hardest part of a three-script system was never the letterforms.

This is the shape that most resembles a curiosity hook while remaining honest, because the
reader knows exactly what the post is about. Her best option when she wants tension.

## Banned, and why, beyond the voice rules

Each of these is banned in `linkedin-content-system.md`. The reasons here are the practical
ones, so the rule is defensible rather than arbitrary.

| Hook | Why it fails for her specifically |
|---|---|
| "I learned something last week that changed how I work." | Pure withholding. Also throws away her only real edge, which is that she can be specific |
| "Ever wondered why some brands feel expensive?" | A question to the reader is the single most recognisable ghostwriter tell. It also invites "no" |
| "Unpopular opinion:" | Announces a stance instead of taking one. Almost always followed by a popular opinion |
| "I was fired. Here's what it taught me." | Manufactured vulnerability. On a profile her employer reads, it is also a needless flare |
| "5 things I learned from..." | Promises a tip list, which she does not write |
| "Let that sink in." | Instructs the reader how to feel |
| A one-word line, then a break, then the real opening | Formatting as a substitute for having something to say |
| Any statistic she has not verified | The LEGO shape, in the most visible sentence of the post |

## Craft technique: write the hook last

The strongest hook is usually already in the draft, sitting in paragraph three, because it is
the sentence she wrote once she had warmed up. Draft the post, then read it looking for the
line that would make a stranger stop, and move it to the front.

This suits her writing in particular, since her posts are structured as recalled thinking and
the sharpest formulation tends to arrive mid-piece.

Two supporting habits:

- **Cut the run-up.** First drafts usually open one or two sentences before the real
  beginning. "Last week I was in Brussels and I visited a place called Tour & Taxis" is the
  run-up. "I stood inside a building in Brussels that used to move the mail of an entire
  continent" is the beginning.
- **Never let the hook be the most interesting sentence in the post.** If it is, the post
  under-delivers, and under-delivery is now the expensive failure.

## The five-part hook test

Run before any draft is handed over. It folds into the handover checklist in `SKILL.md`.

1. **The 140 test.** Under 140 characters, and does the visible snippet stand alone as a
   complete thought? Check where the paragraph break falls.
2. **The withholding test.** Is it interesting because of what it says, or because of what
   it hides? If it hides, rewrite.
3. **The anyone test.** Could any creative director have written this line? If yes, it is not
   hers yet.
4. **The delivery test.** Does the post deliver exactly what the hook implies, no less? An
   overpromise costs dwell time, which is the metric that actually matters.
5. **The two-reader test.** As her boss, then as an EU recruiter. Applies to the hook on its
   own, because for most readers the hook is the whole post.

## Worked examples, for the unwritten posts

Character counts measured. All under the 140 mobile limit.

### Aug 6, Arabic first

| | Hook | Chars | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Most bilingual work in this region is made in English and then moved into Arabic. You can see it immediately. | 109 | Flat contradiction |
| B | I stopped building bilingual layouts in English first. The Arabic was always the part that paid for it. | 103 | Admission |
| C | The question about bilingual work is not whether it translates well. It is whether it was ever made in Arabic. | 110 | Wrong thing broke |

**A is the one to use.** It is the current draft opening, and it is right: flat, specific,
and it makes every bilingual designer in her audience wince in recognition. C is her own
closing line and should stay at the end, where it lands harder.

### Aug 13, the AI shame

| | Hook | Chars | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Someone asked the room who uses AI every day. About a third of the hands stayed down, and I did not believe all of them. | 120 | Standing-somewhere |
| B | About a third of the room would not admit to using AI. The looks the rest of us got were the interesting part. | 110 | Standing-somewhere |
| C | I use Claude at work and I say so out loud. Apparently that is still a strange thing to admit in a room of designers. | 117 | Admission |

**A, with one caution.** "I did not believe all of them" is the sharpest line available and
it is her actual take. It is an opinion about a room she was in, which is safe. It must not
harden into a statistic anywhere in the post.

### Aug 17, the ugly design era

| | Hook | Chars | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Twenty-two years in, I am not always sure that what I am looking at is design. | 78 | Admission |
| **B** | The question is not whether AI can design. It is whether anyone is still editing. | 81 | Wrong thing broke |
| C | We are producing more design than ever and I think most of it is ugly. I want to be fair about why. | 99 | Flat contradiction |

**B.** It is the actual thesis, it earns the provocation without naming anyone's work, and
"is anyone still editing" is a question only someone with 22 years can ask without sounding
bitter. It also solves the accuracy risk flagged for this post: an argument about editing
needs no examples to point at.

Both A and B are well under the limit, so the second sentence of the post can sit in the same
paragraph if it helps.
